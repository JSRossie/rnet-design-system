# VOICE.md — Editorial Voice Standard

**Companion to:** `SYSTEM.md` v1.5
**Document version:** 1.0
**Last updated:** 2026.05.17
**Applies to:** briefings, reports, research documents, memos, status docs — any prose deliverable authored under the JR / R—Net design system.

---

## 0. How This Document Works

This is the writing-voice standard for the JR / R—Net design system. It
removes the recognizable default-LLM voice and replaces it with a
deliberate one: a researcher briefing a reader who is making a hard
decision.

The voice is the prose sibling of the visual system. Both rest on one
premise, stated in `SYSTEM.md` §1 and §12: prefer restraint over
elaboration, and when a choice between adding and removing is even, remove.
`SYSTEM.md` §18 is the short pointer to this document; this is the full
standard.

When **authoring**, hold the rules below as constraints while drafting.
When **revising existing prose**, work the revision checklist in §6.

The voice does not change the analysis, the structure of an argument, or
the facts. It changes how the prose carries them.

---

## 1. Governing Principle

**Disciplined restraint, with rationed and meaningful flourish.**

Every rule below is a corollary of that sentence. When adding or cutting is
a tie, cut. The one counterweight: do not over-correct into voiceless
prose. Text stripped of every tell but given no positive voice reads as
processed, not written, and that is its own tell. The voice must be
present, not merely the absence of bad habits.

---

## 2. The Voice, by Dimension

A writing voice is built from eight dimensions. Each is specified below.

### 2.1 Persona and stance

The writer is a researcher, a briefer, an educator. The writer is never
opinionated and never inserts a personal voice.

- **No self-narration.** Cut any sentence, clause, or label that announces
  the document's own honesty, fairness, balance, rigor, or importance. The
  reader infers rigor from the work. A writer who is honest does not say so.
  - Cut: "The honest read:", "both halves are true and both belong here",
    "the section that matters", "stated fairly", "A note on what this
    briefing is not", "to be clear", "the honest characterization".
- **Balance is structural, not announced.** Achieve balance by giving each
  side its due. Never tell the reader the treatment is balanced.
- **No praising the subject.** Cut editorializing approval ("simple and
  powerful" becomes "simple"). No significance inflation: a fact does not
  "mark a turning point" or sit "at the heart of" anything unless that is
  itself the demonstrated claim.

### 2.2 Address

The writer is an **engaged briefer**. The writer stands close to the
reader, names the reader's situation, and writes in the third person.

- Never "you". Never address the subject of the briefing directly.
- Attribute a decision to whoever owns it, never to a group that does not.
  If a migration decision is the operator's, the prose says "the operator's
  decision", never "the team's".

### 2.3 Diction

- **Ban AI-vocabulary.** Blocklist, non-exhaustive: delve, tapestry,
  pivotal, crucial, underscore, intricate, interplay, vibrant, testament,
  foster, realm, navigate (figurative), landscape (figurative), "evolving
  landscape", showcase, leverage (as a verb), robust, seamless, holistic,
  multifaceted, "stands as", "serves as", "boasts".
- **Plain copulas.** "is", "are", "has". Not "serves as", "stands as",
  "boasts".
- **No vague attributions.** Every opinion carries a named source or is
  cut. Forbidden: "experts argue", "observers note", "it is widely seen
  as", "industry reports suggest".

### 2.4 Syntax

- **No negative parallelism.** Forbidden shapes: "not just X, it's Y"; "not
  a Z but a W"; clipped tail negations ("no guessing", "no wasted motion").
  Rewrite as a plain positive clause.
- **No superficial -ing tails.** Cut trailing participle clauses that
  editorialize instead of stating fact ("...reflecting the program's
  commitment to safety"). State the fact or cut it.
- **Active voice, subject first.**
- **Rule of three is allowed, never enforced.** Keep three elements when
  each earns its place. Never pad to three to complete the pattern. Never
  cut to two to avoid it. Context decides.

### 2.5 Rhythm and pacing

- **Require burstiness.** Mix short and long sentences deliberately. A
  short sentence after two long ones lands. A page of uniform medium
  sentences reads as machine-made.
- **Air carries weight.** Brevity and white space are what make a sharp
  sentence register. This is §2 of `SYSTEM.md` applied to the sentence.

### 2.6 Modality

- **Measured paired characterization is allowed and encouraged.** "Real but
  young." "Deep but recent." This is a judgment, not a hedge. It states
  both sides of one fact in few words.
- **Qualitative lead, specifics in the body.** A topic or opening sentence
  characterizes ("thinner than the capacity sheet implies"). Hard
  specifics, exact dates and counts, belong in the sentences that follow,
  not in the lead.
- **Cut filler and stacked hedges.** "in order to" becomes "to". "due to
  the fact that" becomes "because". "it is important to note that" is cut.
  "could potentially possibly" collapses to one qualifier.
- **State a hard finding plainly, then bound it.** Say the difficult thing
  and let it land. If it needs a bound or a confidence level, give that in
  a separate following sentence. Never use a same-sentence "but" that
  defuses the finding as the reader meets it.

### 2.7 Architecture

- **No global framing bolted onto a local passage.** Decision-ownership
  statements and system-level framing belong in the document's top matter.
  A section intro does local work.
- **No formulaic closers.** Cut "In conclusion", "Overall", "The key
  takeaway", generic upbeat endings, and standalone "Challenges and Future
  Prospects" sections. End on substance.

### 2.8 Mechanics

- **Em-dash: effectively eliminated.** Restructure with periods, colons, or
  parentheses. The genuinely warranted case is rare enough that in practice
  a briefing has none. Introducing an appositive does not warrant one; a
  colon serves. The em-dash has become a recognized AI tell, so removing it
  is also removing a signature.
  - This is a *prose* rule. It does not touch the wordmark "R—Net", whose
    em-dash is a fixed visual mark specified in `SYSTEM.md` §11. One is a
    sentence-level habit; the other is a name. Eliminating the habit leaves
    the name untouched.
- **No italic single-word emphasis.** Build the sentence to land its own
  stress. Italics are for terminology contrast or titles only.
- **Bold only as a label in a genuine parallel series.** A comparison, an
  enumerated factor list. Never as a paragraph's topic or claim sentence.
  The test: read the bolded text alone. If it is a sentence that could be
  true or false, it is a claim, and the bold goes. If it is a name or
  category marking which parallel entry the reader is on, it stays.
- **Headers name content, never the virtue of the treatment.** Title Case.
  No em-dash in headers.
- **Possessive of a noun ending in s uses the apostrophe-only form.**
  James', not James's.

## 3. Structural Choices

### 3.1 Bullets versus prose

Prose is the default. A bullet list is a deliberate exception.

A list is for a **set**; prose is for an **argument**. If reordering the
items loses nothing, a list is fine. If item 2 depends on item 1, joined by
a because, a therefore, a but, or a so, it must be prose. A bullet whose
content is a multi-sentence mini-paragraph wanted to be prose. A section
that is mostly bullets has probably under-written its prose.

### 3.2 The single-sentence paragraph

A sentence given its own paragraph gets white space, and the white space
gives it weight. Use it for a genuine hinge, pivot, or landing.

It is a rationed device, used perhaps once or twice in a major section. A
run of single-sentence paragraphs is web-listicle rhythm and lands nothing.
The isolation has to serve the context and earn its place.

### 3.3 Panache

A document gets two or three deliberate flourishes. No more. Each must
signal something specific. This is the §2.1 single-accent discipline of
`SYSTEM.md` — accent in at most three places — applied to prose.

The flourish is **rhythmic, not figurative**: a confident beat or a precise
reversal ("Vendor A does not match that breadth, and does not try to"), not
a metaphor or an image. Restraint everywhere else is what makes a flourish
land.

## 4. Banned Patterns: Quick Reference

These patterns run dense in unrevised LLM prose. Self-narration of virtue
and reflexive em-dashes are the worst offenders, often several instances
per page. The table is a grep target, not a ranking.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Self-narration of virtue | "The honest read:", "stated fairly" |
| "genuine" as an emphasis crutch | "genuine research identity" |
| Em-dash habit | any reflexive em-dash in prose |
| Bold claim-sentence lead-in | "**The professional side is genuine.**" |
| Negative parallelism | "not a choice, it is the curriculum" |
| Italic single-word emphasis | "what tools they *emphasize*" |
| AI-vocabulary | delve, pivotal, crucial, underscore |
| Vague attribution | "experts argue", "observers note" |
| Formulaic closer | "In conclusion", "The key takeaway" |

## 5. Before and After

The examples use a running subject: an R—Net infrastructure briefing on
whether to migrate a service from one node to another.

**Self-narration removed.**
- Before: "This is not a migration sales document. Section 3 names the
  specific outages the current node has caused. Section 5 gives the
  stay-put option its full and genuine due. The goal is a fair reading, not
  a verdict."
- After: "Section 3 names the outages the current node has caused. Section
  5 makes the case for staying on it and counts the costs of moving."

**Measured characterization kept, specifics out of the lead.**
- Before: "A network is only as good as what it does. The R—Net mesh
  picture is genuinely strong at the core and newer and thinner at the edge
  than the capacity sheet implies, both halves are true and both belong
  here."
- After: "R—Net's mesh is strong at the core. The edge links carrying
  traffic to the newer sites are thinner than the capacity sheet implies.
  The redundancy is real but uneven."

**Em-dash removed by restructuring.**
- Before: "The failover paths, the secondary uplink, the cross-site tunnel,
  the cached static mirror, are the routes traffic takes when the primary
  drops."
- After: "Three routes carry traffic when the primary drops: the secondary
  uplink, the cross-site tunnel, and the cached static mirror. They are the
  system's standing failover plan."

**Address and rhythm: detached default to engaged briefer.**
- Before: "The operator has a confirmed maintenance window for the core
  switch on Friday. The team is deciding whether to proceed or defer."
- After: "This is the decision in front of the operator. There is a
  confirmed maintenance window for the core switch on Friday, and a second
  window the following Tuesday if this one is passed. Both are real, and
  both are the operator's to choose."

**Panache: flat to one disciplined flourish.**
- Before: "Vendor B's coverage is wider. The honest characterization:
  Vendor B is a broad mid-tier provider; Vendor A is a narrow provider with
  one strong region."
- After: "Vendor B's coverage map is wider than Vendor A's, and its SLA is
  firmer: a 99.95% guarantee, a four-hour response. Vendor A does not match
  that breadth, and does not try to. Its strength is concentrated in the
  one region where R—Net's traffic is heaviest."

**Argument moved from bullets to prose.**
- Before: a list of "migrating costs more" / "the latency gain is small" /
  "therefore the numbers do not justify moving" / "the case rests on
  reliability".
- After: "Migration's cost is real, and it does not pay for itself on
  latency. Moving the service costs roughly three weeks of operator time,
  while the latency gain is under 8ms. The arithmetic does not bridge that
  gap. If the migration case holds, it holds on reliability and the cleaner
  failover path."

**Headers: virtue to content.**
- "Reliability, The Honest Summary" becomes "Reliability".
- "What Staying Put Produces, Stated Fairly" becomes "What Staying Put
  Produces".
- "The Honest Warning First" becomes "Before the Comparison".

## 6. Revision Checklist

Apply in order when revising existing prose.

1. Grep for the §2.3 AI-vocabulary blocklist and for em-dashes. Reduce both.
2. Find self-narration: any sentence announcing the document's honesty,
   fairness, balance, or importance. Cut it.
3. Find bold paragraph lead-ins. Classify each as claim or label. Demote
   every claim into plain prose.
4. Find each paragraph's topic sentence. Move hard specifics out of it and
   into the body.
5. Read each section for negative parallelism, superficial -ing tails,
   vague attributions, and filler. Rewrite.
6. Read for rhythm. Vary sentence length. Add short beats where the prose
   has gone uniform.
7. Check every header: content not virtue, Title Case, no em-dash.
8. Check every bullet list: if it carries an argument, convert it to prose.
9. Confirm the document has at most two or three flourishes, and that each
   is rhythmic rather than figurative.
10. Read the result aloud. A sentence that is hard to say is usually hard
    to read.
